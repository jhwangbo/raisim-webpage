Explosion Effects ​

raisin::RayraiWindow draws the explosions and projectiles of the world it shows, with no code beyond setting them off:

  • Explosions (World::addExplosion() and explosive projectiles): a fireball that flashes white-hot, swells and cools through orange to dull red and into soot; a cloud of smoke that billows, rises and spreads for several seconds, lit by the sun from above and by the fire from within; sparks and debris thrown out of it; and a flash of light on its surroundings. Fireball and smoke are heaps of uneven lobes, laid out anew for every explosion and bent by noise, with ragged, wispy rims.
  • Ground bursts (surfaceBurst or a crater) also throw up a column of earth and dust under the smoke, dust that rolls out along the ground, clods of earth that arc out and fall back, and leave a scorch mark. A charge at or under the surface of one of the window's water bodies throws up a column of spray and mist instead.
  • Muzzle flashes where projectiles are launched, sized by the cube root of the projectile's kinetic energy (a tank gun's at full size, a machine gun's a sixth of it), also for a round that launched and came down between two frames, tracers behind them in flight and sparks where an inert projectile hits.
  • Fires (addFireEffect()): flickering flames and a column of black smoke, for a burning wreck or a fuel spill.

Sizes and durations scale with the cube root of the charge, as the blast does. Smoke and dust drift with the wind of the world's ambient fluid (World::setAmbientFluid()), and sparks, clods and debris drag against it. Smoke, dust and debris are lit by the window's main light and sky as its meshes are, so they keep their color in a photoreal scene lit by a strong sun.

addExplosionEffect() draws an explosion that is not in the world, for example one a game computes itself:

cpp
raisin::ExplosionEffect effect;
effect.position = {4.0f, 0.0f, 0.3f};
effect.chargeMass = 2.0f;   // kg of TNT: sets the size and duration
effect.onGround = true;     // column of earth and dust, clods and a scorch mark
effect.dustColor = {0.19f, 0.13f, 0.075f};  // the soil it throws up (linear albedo)
viewer.addExplosionEffect(effect);
ExplosionEffect fieldDefaultMeaning
positionoriginCenter of the charge (m).
chargeMass1 kgTNT-equivalent mass; sets the size and duration.
onGroundfalseA ground burst: earth, dust, clods and a scorch mark.
smokeColor(0.34, 0.32, 0.30)Albedo of the smoke (linear RGB).
dustColor(0.26, 0.22, 0.17)Albedo of the earth and dust of a ground burst.
inWaterfalseA burst at a water surface: spray and mist instead of earth.

addFireEffect() starts a fire that burns for a while:

cpp
raisin::FireEffect fire;
fire.position = {2.0f, -3.0f, 0.4f};  // base of the flames (m)
fire.size = 0.9f;                     // radius of the flames (m); the smoke column scales with it
fire.duration = 30.0f;                // seconds it burns; it dies down over the last fifth
viewer.addFireEffect(fire);

Its flames and smoke are particles, so a fire costs far less than a ray-marched explosion: a dozen burning wrecks add a few hundred quads to a frame.

The window compiles the shaders of the effects when it first draws one, and the first scorch mark switches post-processing to the variant that draws projected decals. A game calls prepareExplosionEffects() after creating the window: it compiles those shaders, and the next frame draws the decal variant once with a hidden decal, so that the browser also builds its GPU pipeline then. Without it, the first shot stalls a frame while they compile, for about a second in the browser.

Settings ​

setExplosionEffectSettings() takes an ExplosionEffectSettings:

FieldDefaultMeaning
enabledtrueDraw explosions, muzzle flashes, impacts and tracers.
sizeScale1Multiplies the size of fireballs and smoke clouds.
smokeLifetime8 sHow long the smoke of a 1 kg charge lasts.
marchSteps32Samples per pixel through fire and smoke (8 to 64): fewer cost less and look grainier.
volumeDownsample2Fire and smoke are marched at 1/n of the image's width and height (1 to 4) and blended into it along its depth, so that they stay behind nearer objects: 2 costs about a third of 1, and 3 about half of 2.
sparkstrueThrow sparks, debris and dust.
flashLighttrueLight the surroundings with the brightest fireball.
scorchMarkstrueLeave scorch marks under ground bursts.
scorchLifetime60 sHow long a scorch mark stays before it fades.
tracerstrueDraw glowing streaks behind projectiles in flight.
useWorldTimetrueAnimate with the world's time, so effects pause and rewind with the simulation and sensor images are reproducible; false animates with the wall clock, for a window whose world does not advance.
smokeColor(0.12, 0.115, 0.11)Albedo of the smoke of the world's explosions (linear RGB).
dustColor(0.26, 0.22, 0.17)Albedo of the earth and dust their ground bursts throw up; match it to the ground (sand, snow, concrete).

The world's explosions take their colors from the settings. A charge over water that is under the surface of a water body the window draws (setWaterBody(), simulated rivers included), or less than half the cube root of its mass (in metres, kg) above it, bursts as spray.

How they are drawn ​

The fireball and smoke of each explosion are ray-marched through the sphere that bounds them, after the opaque scene: each ray stops at the scene's surface, read from a copy of its depth, so the fire and smoke wrap around the ground and the objects in them. Their density comes from a small tiling 3D noise texture over three octaves, shaped into billowing puffs; the smoke is lit by the sun through a few shadow samples toward it, by the sky and by the fire's glow. The main light falls on it as on a Lambertian surface (its color over pi), so smoke and dust are as bright as ground of the same albedo. A ground burst's smoke stands on a column from the ground, narrower at its foot, and a thin layer of dust spreads along the ground around it; the lower the medium, the more it takes the dust's color. The result is tone-mapped like the scene's meshes, so it glows into bloom where post-processing has it.

Sparks, flames, clods, debris, dust and smoke particles and tracers are camera-facing quads depth-tested against the scene. The lit ones are drawn farthest first, then the glowing ones; the soft ones fade out over a depth proportional to their size where they meet the scene, read from the same depth copy, so a dust puff does not end in a hard line on the ground. The effects appear in every camera that draws the scene's color, sensor cameras included.

The flash is one additional light (AdditionalLight, a point light without shadows) and the scorch marks are projected decals, at most four. The window adds them after the application's lights and decals for each render and takes them away when the render ends, so the application's lists and their indices never change. They count toward the limits of 16 lights and 8 decals and are skipped when those are full.

The effects follow the physics snapshots the window shows (PhysicsSnapshot::explosions, projectiles, projectileHits, launches and wind), so they also work with applyPhysicsSnapshot() on a render thread. Each effect belongs to the explosion, hit or launch that caused it and is drawn from it alone, sparks and puffs included, so a replay draws the same picture. A checkpoint restored to before an explosion drops its effect, one restored to while it was still burning or smoking draws it again, and an explosion replayed after a restore gets its effect even when it reuses an identifier, at its own place. Changing useWorldTime keeps the effects in progress at the age they had. Positions, charges, sizes and durations that are not finite are refused with std::invalid_argument; orthographic cameras draw the fire and smoke along parallel rays like the rest of the scene. Effects removed with clearExplosionEffects() stay removed. In the browser the effects draw through the WebGPU backend like the rest of the scene.

The fireball and smoke cost a ray march over the pixels they cover, so rayrai keeps the march short and coarse and blends it into the image:

  • Each ray takes about marchSteps steps across the volume's bounding sphere, so rays that cross only its edge or meet the scene early take fewer.
  • The march fetches the noise only where it can change the density, so the empty reaches of a volume's bounding sphere cost less than its cloud.
  • It runs at half the image's width and height (volumeDownsample). Each marched pixel stops at the nearest scene depth among the image pixels it covers, and neighbouring marched pixels start their rays at interleaved fractions of a step.
  • Each image pixel blends the four by four nearest marched pixels, weighted by distance and by how close the depth each one stopped at is to its own. This averages the interleaved starts into smooth smoke instead of grain, and keeps smoke behind a tank from spilling over its edges or over a gun barrel or branch thinner than a marched pixel.

On an RTX 5070 at 1600 x 900 with 32 steps, a smoke screen across half the view adds 0.85 ms to the frame (0.5 ms at a third of the resolution, which Tank Survival uses). The worst case is a camera inside overlapping clouds: looking into the dust of a dozen ground bursts (rayrai_explosion_volume_benchmark) adds 1.8 ms, 0.9 ms at a third of the resolution and 5.9 ms at full resolution. Fewer marchSteps make it cheaper and grainier.
